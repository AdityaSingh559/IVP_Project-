Count batteries and get the total from one photo

Put the batteries and a credit card on a sheet of white paper and take a photo from above. The table around the paper does not matter: the paper is found and cropped automatically. Each battery is measured in millimetres against the card, named from the catalogue, counted and priced. It uses classical image processing only, with no AI or machine learning.

What it does

Six parts, working one after the other.

01

Auto-crop

Finds the white paper in the photo, cuts away the table around it and straightens it with a perspective warp.

02

Scale

Finds the credit card (always 85.60 x 53.98 mm) and uses its four corners to turn pixels into true millimetres.

03

Measure

Sorts each object into cylinder (AA, AAA) or round cell (CR2016, LR44) by shape, and measures its length and diameter.

04

Identify

Names each battery from the catalogue sizes, with a confidence. Unclear objects are flagged, never guessed.

05

Leakage check

Looks for white crust and green or rust corrosion at the ends of every battery, and warns you about any that are leaking.

06

Count and total

How many of each battery, times its price, and the grand total. Download as PDF, CSV or JPG.

How well does it work?

Tested on drawn scenes so far. Upload your own photo and check the counts by eye.

On drawn test photos with the real sizes (4 AA, 4 AAA, 2 CR2016, 2 LR44 and a card on A4 paper, on a wooden table, camera tilted up to 7 deg; shadows, uneven light, blur, noise and JPEG added, 3 versions, one with two AA cells touching):

  • ✓ the paper was found and cropped in 3 of 3
  • ✓ every battery counted and named correctly in 3 of 3 (total ₹260 each time)
  • ✓ sizes within about 0.5 mm; confidence 79 to 99%

Drawn scenes show that the method works. Accuracy on phone photos still has to be checked.

Limits

  • The credit card must be in the photo, flat, not touching anything. A coloured card works best; a white card on white paper is hard to see.
  • Batteries must lie flat: AA and AAA on their side, coin and button cells face up. A cell standing on its end looks like a small circle.
  • Leave a small gap between batteries. Two cells that touch are split or counted by their combined size, but a gap is always safer.
  • Shiny metal can reflect the light. Avoid a bright lamp straight above; daylight from a window is best.

Leakage check

Check old batteries before you use them. Put them on white paper and take a close photo from above. Every battery is checked at its ends (AA, AAA) or on its face and rim (coin and button cells) for leaks. A credit card is optional: with one, the deposit is measured in mm².

White crust

Alkaline leakage (potassium carbonate). Found as bright, almost grey and rough pixels: texture from the median residual.

Green-blue corrosion

Copper salts on old contacts. Found by colour slicing in HSV, ignoring the battery's own wrapper colour.

Rust brown

Corroded steel casing. Colour slicing in HSV, again leaving out the body colour.

Not a leak: glare

Shiny metal reflects light and is brighter than the paper. A matte crust cannot be, so glare is found with a top-hat and ignored.
